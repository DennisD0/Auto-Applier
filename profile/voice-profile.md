## What transfers to job applications, and what doesn't

The source guide is written for coursework. Per the agreed rule — carry the constants, drop
the academic register — the following are **course-specific and do not apply here**: the
four-step text-comment method, the 2,000-word target, no-thinker-overlap, textbook-only
sourcing, podcast-outline discipline, exit-ticket phrasing, draft-to-professor deadlines.

Everything below transfers.

## Hard formatting rules — absolute

- **No em-dashes, and no hyphens used as punctuation.** Anywhere. He has enforced this
  repeatedly.
- **No colons anywhere in the body.**
- **No bullet points or numbered lists** inside prose.
- **No bold or italics for emphasis mid-paragraph.**
- **No abbreviations.** "Book of the Month", never "BOTM". Corrected explicitly.
- Plain text throughout. No decorative punctuation.

## Sentences

**Varied length, deliberately.** Longer connected sentences joined with "and", "since",
"which", "because" for explanation and reasoning. Shorter punchy sentences to land a point.
The variation is the point — it is what keeps the prose from reading as generated.

Not short declarative fragments strung together. Not uniformly long either.

## Register

Plain and direct. Not formal, not academic-sounding, not performing sophistication.

His own framing, which generalizes almost perfectly to an application answer: *sounds like a
student who understands the material and is explaining it clearly, not performing
sophistication.* For job applications, read that as someone who has done the work and can
explain it plainly, rather than someone signaling competence through vocabulary.

Technical vocabulary only where genuinely needed and naturally earned.

## Structure

- **Open directly with the point.** No throat-clearing, no restating the prompt, no structural
  announcement.
- **No signposting.** Not "In this essay I will", not "First… Second… Third…".
- **No section subtitles** beyond, at most, one plain line restating the question.
- **Conclusions do real work** — extend the reasoning, make a final claim, or reframe what was
  established. Never a recap.
- **Narrative over labels.** He rejected a draft structured as "Most aligned is… Least aligned
  is…" in favor of a story. He does not want the shape of the question visible in the answer.
- Citations, where relevant, are inline and conversational: "In chapter 3, it says…" Never
  parenthetical or footnote-style.

## Avoiding AI-detection tells

His drafts have been flagged before, and he named the reasons: formulaic flow, predictable
syntax, robotic formality, repeated wrapper phrases.

This matters more for job applications than it did for coursework — recruiters and ATS
vendors increasingly screen for generated text, and a flagged application is dead on arrival
with no feedback.

**Prioritize:**
- Natural rhythm through genuinely varied sentence length
- No repeated openers or transitional templates across paragraphs
- Short punchy sentences breaking up longer runs
- Language that sounds like a person reasoning through something in real time

**Never:**
- Start two sentences in the same paragraph with the same word or phrase
- Use passive constructions that distance the argument from its claim
- Use jargon decoratively rather than precisely
- Reuse a sentence shape that appeared earlier in the same answer

## Words and constructions to avoid

`leverage` · `passionate about` · `delve` · `spearheaded` · `deeply` · `resonates with me` ·
`I'm excited about the opportunity to` · `In this essay/post I will` · `Moreover` ·
`Furthermore` · `That said,` as a paragraph opener · `First… Second… Third…` · any em-dash ·
any hyphen as punctuation · any colon in the body · any abbreviated company name

## Multi-question applications — one story per question

When an application asks several free-text questions, **each answer uses a distinct project or
example.** Never reuse the same anchor twice in one application.

Demonstrated on the Zenyt.ai Product Owner Intern application: "why this internship" was
anchored in MavenStudio and the AI-builder identity, while "a project you were genuinely
driven to do" used En Hakkore Cafe — a different story deliberately.

Default anchor when only one is needed: **MavenStudio**. It is the narrative center and
connects most naturally to product ownership, client communication, prioritization, and
AI-building credibility.

## Register can shift when the format demands it

Casual and conversational is the default. **Formal when the format calls for it** — the Zenyt
application was a full email to a hiring address and was written formally while keeping his
natural voice. Formal means no slang and proper structure; it does not mean abandoning the
punctuation rules or the narrative preference.

## When run-ons get flagged

Break into shorter complete thoughts while preserving the connective style. Do not chop into
staccato fragments; the conjunction-driven flow is the point.

## Style governs sound, never substance

Matching his voice does not license a claim absent from `master-resume.md`. A fabricated claim
in a convincing voice is *worse* than one in a generic voice, because it is less likely to be
caught. The anti-fabrication check runs on drafted free-text exactly as on resume bullets.

The calibration sample earns its credibility from one specific true thing about MavenStudio,
not from stylistic confidence. Copy that pattern.
